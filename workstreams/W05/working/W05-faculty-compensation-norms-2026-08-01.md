## (b) NARRATIVE BY CATEGORY

### 1. Published honorarium / per-session rates

**The only fully public, current, multi-country day-rate cards that are directly usable for Nigeria, Ghana and Pakistan are the UN and ILO tables.** Both are official, published, and country-differentiated. The ILO table (effective 1 January 2026) prices Nigeria higher than Pakistan and South Africa — Nigeria's published band runs from USD 125 at the lowest complexity/experience combination to USD 642 at the highest; Pakistan runs USD 108–555; Ghana USD 123–634 ([ILO PDF](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2025-11/ILO%20Excoll%20Fee%20Range%20Table%20-%20effective%201%20Jan%202026%20-%20EN_0.pdf), accessed 2026-08-01, [OWN][PDF]).

The UN OHR table (OHR/PG/2025/2, effective 17 January 2025) is not country-differentiated and is higher at the top: Band E is USD 1,000–1,100 per day, Band A USD 190–260 per day ([policy.un.org PDF](https://policy.un.org/sites/default/files/2025-01/OHR_PG_2025_2_consultant_contractor_fee_range.pdf), accessed 2026-08-01, [OWN][PDF]).

**WHO's own APW rate card is NOT FOUND as a published schedule** (see register, §d).

**University guest-lecturer honorarium schedules that publish actual numbers are rare, and the ones that exist are small.** Boston University MET publishes USD 50/100/150 for 1/2/3-hour lectures ([bu.edu/met](https://www.bu.edu/met/?p=79742)) — i.e. **USD 50 per hour**. Pondicherry University (India) publishes **Rs 1,500 per lecture with a Rs 50,000/month ceiling** in a notification dated 27 October 2025 ([PDF](https://www.pondiuni.edu.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Re-notification-Interviewforguestfaculty-PUKaraikalcampus-28102025.pdf)). This is the clearest current South Asian public-institution guest-faculty rate located.

**Professional-society / CPD speaker honoraria in the US cluster at USD 500–2,000 per event**, tiered by whether the speaker is local or visiting (Marshall University, February 2025, [PDF](https://jcesom.marshall.edu/media/63674/speaker-and-honoraria-policy.pdf)). TTUHSC's "zero to $2,000 per activity" (October 2023, [STALE]) confirms the ceiling but explicitly permits **zero** — CME policy in the US frequently contemplates unpaid faculty.

**A published rate card specifically for virtual teaching exists in Bangladesh.** The Finance Ministry issued revised honorarium rates in June 2026 for online (Zoom) seminars and workshops: keynote presenter BDT 4,500 per session, moderator BDT 4,000, discussant BDT 4,000 (max 3), rapporteur BDT 2,500 (max 2), participant BDT 1,000 ([Daily Star, 20 June 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/business/news/govt-revises-honorarium-rates-online-seminars-workshops-4203446); [21 June 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/business/economy/news/honorarium-raised-online-seminars-workshops-4203766), [3P]). Note the direction of travel: during COVID (2021 directive) online **participants** were paid at half rate; the 2026 directive restores full rates. This is a South Asian government treating a virtual session as worth the same as an in-person one.

### 3. Coaching, mentoring and supervision rates

Three distinct payment architectures are visible, and they are not interchangeable.

**(i) Per-session payment for teaching contact — the closest analogue to your monthly live faculty slot.** Australia's PIP Teaching Payment is **AUD 200 for each 3-hour teaching session**, maximum two sessions per GP per day, minimum session length 3 hours ([PracticeAssist fact sheet V3, Aug 2020 PDF](http://www.practiceassist.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/PIP-Teaching-Payment-Fact-Sheet-V3-200812.pdf), [3P][PDF], **[STALE]**). The Sax Institute review records this rate as having been **AUD 100 per three-hour session, doubled to AUD 200 from January 2015**, with a rural/remote loading of up to 50% ([Sax Institute PDF](https://www.saxinstitute.org.au/wp-content/uploads/The-costs-and-benefits-of-providing-undergraduate-student-clinical-place....pdf), December 2014, **[STALE]**).

**> YOUR ARITHMETIC:** AUD 200 ÷ 3 hours = **AUD 66.67 per hour** of teaching contact. Inputs: the AUD 200 rate and the 3-hour session definition, both from the PracticeAssist fact sheet above.

**(ii) Per-student-per-day placement subsidy — the closest analogue to your 40-hour observership.** Victoria's subsidy scheme paid **AUD 60 per student per day** (nursing/midwifery) and **AUD 35 per student per day** (allied health), explicitly framed as maximum subsidies not expected to cover costs (Sax Institute, December 2014, **[STALE]**). The current Victorian standardised schedule of maximum chargeable placement fees exists but **could not be read — see register**.

The English equivalent operates as a tariff paid to the placement provider, not to the individual supervisor: **£5,519 per FTE plus market forces factor** for non-medical clinical placements (40.8 weeks), **£34,355 + MFF** per undergraduate medical student, **£13,337 + MFF + salary contribution** per postgraduate medical trainee, effective 1 April 2024 ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/healthcare-education-and-training-tariff-2024-to-2025/education-and-training-tariffs-2024-to-2025), [OWN]).

**(iii) Retainer/grant payment for a named supervisor role — the closest analogue to your small-group coach.** Severn Primary Care (NHS) publishes: GP clinical/educational supervisor **£770 per month** from 1 April 2022; GP educational supervisor **£500 per year** or pro rata; F2 clinical supervisor **£3,264.80 per 4-month placement**; educational supervisor CPD allowance **£750 per year**; and a calendar table entry for a GP Supervision Trainer Grant of **£816.20 per month** ([primarycare.severndeanery.nhs.uk](https://primarycare.severndeanery.nhs.uk/training/faculty/faculty-payments/), [OWN], **[STALE]** — base rate dated 2022). Two other NHS deanery pages carrying the same data (NW PGMD, HEE East of England) were **blocked by bot detection** — see register.

**(iv) US preceptor incentives — small, and demonstrably weak as a motivator.** Michigan's MDHHS pilot paid **USD 500 to USD 1,500** as a flat stipend per preceptor for 120 to 323+ hours committed across two semesters ([MDHHS report PDF](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/-/media/Project/Websites/mdhhs/Doing-Business-with-MDHHS/Health-Care-Providers/Nursing-Programs/Clinical_Preceptor_Pilot_Program_Report.pdf), payments processed 17–20 September 2025).

**> YOUR ARITHMETIC (Michigan implied hourly rate).** Inputs: the five payment/hours bands from the MDHHS report. $500 ÷ 120 h = $4.17/h; $500 ÷ 170 h = $2.94/h; $750 ÷ 171 h = $4.39/h; $1,000 ÷ 222 h = $4.50/h; $1,250 ÷ 273 h = $4.58/h; $1,500 ÷ 323 h = $4.64/h. **Implied range: USD 2.94 to USD 4.64 per supervised hour.** This is a token, not a market rate — and the report's own findings confirm it behaved like one.

### 4. External examiner and assessor fees

**West African, Nigerian, Ghanaian and Pakistani colleges do not publish examiner remuneration.** This is a clean negative finding and it is documented in §d.

Essex additionally documents that UG examiner fees are taxed at source via PAYE while PGT fees are not — a real administrative consideration for a cross-border faculty panel.
- **Kenya, per-candidate and capped.** University of Nairobi: external examiner honorarium **US$50** for 1–20 candidates, **US$1 per candidate** thereafter capped at **US$150**, plus a daily allowance of **KSh 7,500** for meals and incidentals and mileage/airfare; internal moderator **US$300 per year** (US$150 per semester). Document is **undated** and cites a 1985 statute ([uonbi.ac.ke PDF](https://academics.uonbi.ac.ke/sites/default/files/centraladmin/academics/Examinations%20Guide%20II.pdf)).

**UK royal colleges: NOT FOUND as a published rate.** The BMA's *The Doctor* (16 January 2024, Ben Ireland) reports the RCGP citing "the expense of paying for locums to cover examiners (who are practising GPs)" as a cost driver — establishing that examiners are backfilled rather than unpaid — but **discloses no figure**. The same article notes that medical royal college staff who design and run exams "often do so in their own time, without remuneration or recognition" ([thedoctor.bma.org.uk](https://thedoctor.bma.org.uk/articles/life-at-work/the-exam-question/), [3P], accessed 2026-08-01).

### 5. What proportion of faculty are unpaid or volunteer — the direct answer

**The best single number: 81% volunteer.** The CoPPRR study of 26 community preceptors across seven geographically diverse US academic health centres found **21 (81%) were volunteers, 5 (19%) were paid**, and 4 (15%) precepted as an employment requirement (Minor S, Huffman M, Lewis PR, Kost A, Prunuske J. *Fam Med.* 2019;51(5):389-398, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22454/FamMed.2019.937544), via [journals.stfm.org](https://journals.stfm.org/familymedicine/2019/may/minor-2018-0358/), [3P], accessed 2026-08-01) — **[STALE]**, small sample.

**Corroborated at scale, more recently:** NC AHEC's January 2025 report found **only 50% of North Carolina preceptors and 41% of preceptors from nearby states reported receiving compensation**, and **only 40% of surveyed practice sites** reported receiving financial compensation for precepting. The report states financial compensation ranked lowest as an important motivator at 19%, "likely reflecting that most preceptors are not receiving substantial (or any) financial compensation" ([ncahec.net PDF](https://www.ncahec.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/AHEC-Report-on-Preceptors-02.03.25-1.pdf), [OWN][PDF]).

**So: in US community-based health professions teaching, roughly half to four-fifths of faculty are unpaid.** No equivalent proportion figure was located for African, South Asian or LMIC programmes — see register.

### 6. Faculty as a share of the cost base

**One clean study.** Activity-based costing of the School of Health, Alborz University of Medical Sciences (data collected March 2018 – March 2019): **"65% for wages and salary, 26% for depreciation of building and equipment, and 9% for consumable goods and services,"** with total annual cost per student of **USD 4,778** (Pouragha B, Tayebi Arasteh M, Zarei E, Abdolahi M, Sheikhbardsiri H. *J Educ Health Promot.* 2020;9(1):165, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4103/jehp.jehp_778_19), via [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/8162b4025f4e448ab23a3de3f37f5ca8), [3P], accessed 2026-08-01) — **[STALE]**.

**And one clean statement that the field does not know.** The definitive scoping review on eLearning costs in health professions education screened 7,344 articles and found only 42 with usable cost data. Of the excluded studies, **103 were excluded specifically "because of lack of detailed information regarding costs"** — they "referred to cost in ways either indicating cost favorability or unfavorability, but without data to support findings." The authors conclude: *"Despite a perception that eLearning is more cost-effective than face-to-face instruction, there is not yet sufficient evidence to assert this conclusively."* The review found only **5 cost-effectiveness analysis studies** on eLearning in the whole literature. Reported cost per learner ranged from **USD 0.04 to USD 1,782**; several pure online courses came in **below USD 68 per learner**, but the authors warn repeatedly that faculty and production time were routinely omitted from those figures (Ellaway/Bezuidenhout et al., *JMIR Med Educ.* 2021;7(1):e13681, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/13681)). According to PubMed. **[STALE]** by publication year, but no successor review located.

**The honest headline for question 6: there is no published, defensible figure for "faculty as % of a hybrid health-education programme's cost base."** The best proxies are the Alborz 65% (all salaries, a residential school), the ACHS FY2020 17.4% assessment-cost line, and my IHI 44.8–53.7% staff-cost arithmetic. All three measure different things.

## (c) VOLUNTEER-FACULTY SUSTAINABILITY EVIDENCE

### C1. The Peoples-uni case — the closest structural analogue, and it closed

**Peoples-uni closed in March 2021**, "following transfer of all its courses to NextGenU.org — to whom enquiries for further study should be made." Founded 2006; operated 2006–2021; "volunteer expert tutors from 55 countries"; over 100 countries reached, **70% from Africa**; 159 master's graduates; ~7,000 open-course enrolments ([peoples-uni.org](https://peoples-uni.org/), [OWN], accessed 2026-08-01).

**The volunteer conversion rates are documented and they are poor.** From the foundational paper (Heller RF, *Hum Resour Health* 2009;7:43, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1478-4491-7-43); full text retrieved via PubMed Central, accessed 2026-08-01). According to PubMed:

- *"After the publication of various papers and presentations at meetings as well as personal appeals to colleagues and networks, more than 100 people volunteered to help with course development."*
- *"**A majority of those who volunteered, did not in the end make a contribution to the development process.** We have not formally investigated the reasons for this, but lack of familiarity with the ICT system, which was perceived to be complex, and lack of clarity in the instructions for its use may have contributed."*
- *"To date, 46 people have actively contributed to course module development."* Of these 46, 26 were from the UK and 22 from other countries.
- On delivery: *"Some **29 tutors agreed to act as online facilitators, and 25 have been active**. Each course module has one general facilitator to oversee the process, and each topic has one facilitator, who thus may have a role with very limited time commitment."*

**> YOUR ARITHMETIC (Peoples-uni volunteer conversion).** Inputs are the quoted sentences above. Development: 46 active out of "more than 100" who volunteered = **at most 46% conversion from expression of interest to actual contribution**. Delivery: 25 active out of 29 who agreed = **86% conversion**, but note the paper's own qualifier that a topic facilitator "may have a role with very limited time commitment."

**The author named the risk explicitly, in 2009, twelve years before closure.** From the same paper's five listed challenges:

- *"First is the identification of volunteers... **The sustainability of the initiative will depend on our ability to maintain this volunteer workforce.**"*
- *"Third is accreditation of the academic awards, **which has so far eluded us**... at present the awards are made solely by the Peoples Open Access Education Initiative. Credibility of the awards may depend on our ability to achieve accreditation by other organizations."*
- *"Fourth is **the business model, which relies on volunteer activity and a very low enrolment fee. It remains to be seen if this is sustainable.**"*
- *"The fifth challenge is sustainability, which depends on our ability to meet each of the previous challenges."*

**The 2018 independent evaluation confirmed the accreditation challenge was still unresolved.** A mixed-methods evaluation of 71 of 119 master's graduates (60% response) across 31 countries found strong evidence of goal achievement and student impact, but concluded: *"Challenges are to find a way to **accredit the programme to ensure its sustainability**."* Accreditation through partnerships with local universities was the students' own recommendation (Sridharan S, Bondy M, Nakaima A, Heller RF. *Health Res Policy Syst.* 2018;16:106, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12961-018-0381-2), published 12 November 2018, [3P]). According to PubMed. Notably, **this evaluation did not investigate volunteer recruitment or retention at all** — the sustainability discourse had shifted entirely to accreditation.

**The lesson is not "volunteers failed."** Peoples-uni ran for fifteen years on volunteers and produced 159 master's graduates. The lesson is that **the volunteer model survived; the un-accredited, un-monetised institution around it did not.** Both parts of the challenge list — the volunteer workforce and the accreditation/business model — had to hold, and the second gave way first.

### C2. Preceptor and clinical-teacher attrition — what actually causes departure

**Retention is around 85% per year in a well-run programme, and first-year faculty are the fragile cohort.** A survey of 1,164 longitudinal integrated clerkship preceptors at a single US institution (83% response, n=965) found a **preceptor retention rate of 84.5% (816/968)**. Most common reasons for leaving: needing a break (30.1%), leaving clinical practice (20.1%), decreased clinical time (19.3%). **"Specialty, participation in faculty development and perceived ease/difficulty of assessment forms were not associated with retention; only being a first-year preceptor was shown to have significant negative association (p = 0.006)."** For those returning, the top reason was the longitudinal relationship with students (66%). The authors conclude decisions were *"driven primarily by personal or structural factors rather than dissatisfaction with the curriculum"* and that **"first-year faculty are at greatest risk for attrition and represent a key target for intervention"** (Adams JE, Bosma G, Lockspeiser T. *Clin Teach.* 2026;23(3):e70404, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/tct.70404)). According to PubMed. *The Wiley full text returned HTTP 403; abstract read via PubMed.*

**Time — not money — is the binding constraint, and 41.5% are already thinking about quitting.** A survey of 228 of 376 family physicians (60.6% response) found: financial compensation ranked **last as an initial motivator at 2.9%**; 32.3% of current preceptors said salary would encourage them to continue; **80% of current preceptors identified "dedicated time for teaching without impacting productivity" as necessary to continue**; 50% of former preceptors cited time demands as their reason for stopping; and **41.5% of current preceptors had considered discontinuing clinical teaching**. Intrinsic motivators dominated: love of teaching 76.6%, professional duty 67.8%, student relationships 58.5% (Mechling D, Brougham H, Rodriguez C, Kendrick J, Johnson M. *PRiMER.* 2025;9:67, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22454/PRiMER.2025.863726), via [journals.stfm.org](https://journals.stfm.org/primer/2025/johnson-2025-0062/), [3P], accessed 2026-08-01).

**Teaching itself is a burnout vector.** NC AHEC cites a 2024 national AHEC survey finding that **58% of preceptors believe precepting contributes to burnout** ([ncahec.net PDF](https://www.ncahec.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/AHEC-Report-on-Preceptors-02.03.25-1.pdf)). Separately, 97% of schools reported needing more preceptor sites; demand for preceptors in North Carolina grew nearly 40% since 2014; schools dropped an average of 1.3 sites and 1.8 sites stopped accepting students in the preceding 12 months while adding 4.1 new sites. Critically for capacity planning: **88% of responding sites indicated they could take more students** — 56% could add 1–5, 32% could add 6+.

**Paying does not fix it — the Michigan natural experiment.** Of 274 outreach attempts, 60 preceptors accepted the incentive (42 existing, 18 new), 43 declined, 154 did not respond — a **~21% conversion rate**. The killer finding: **"while 88% of respondents reported that the financial incentive had not influenced or had a slight influence on their participation, nearly 67% stated they preferred monetary compensation."** Barriers included organisational policies prohibiting external compensation, ethical concerns and tax implications ([MDHHS report PDF](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/-/media/Project/Websites/mdhhs/Doing-Business-with-MDHHS/Health-Care-Providers/Nursing-Programs/Clinical_Preceptor_Pilot_Program_Report.pdf)).

**Motivation is intrinsic and consistent across specialties.** Among 26 of 38 dermatology volunteer clinical faculty surveyed (68% response), the top-ranked motivators were *"opportunity to be helpful to others"* (100%), *"providing service to the field of dermatology"* (96%) and *"enjoyment of teaching"* (96%). **"Extrinsic rewards such as career advancement and increased income were rated as least important."** Significant barriers: limited time for travel and teaching, and credentialing. Proposed facilitators: schedule flexibility, teaching supplies, **streamlined credentialing** (Delgado F, Immaneni S, MacKelfresh JB, Yeung H. *Arch Dermatol Res.* 2023;315(10):2995-2998, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00403-023-02727-x)). According to PubMed. **[STALE]**.

### C3. The LMIC-specific signal

**In an LMIC pre-service programme, low pay for teachers and preceptors is named as a primary threat.** A 2025 mixed-methods assessment of three IMC-supported midwifery schools and associated clinical sites in South Sudan found the programme overall strong, but identified as key challenges: *"a shortage of qualified clinical preceptors, **low compensation of teachers and preceptors**, inadequate financial support from the Ministry of Health, overcrowding at some clinical sites and the impact of insecurity on education"* (Perera SM, Isa GP, Sebushishe A, Parker E, Ali J, Casey SE. *Nurse Educ Pract.* 2025;88:104540, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nepr.2025.104540)). According to PubMed.

**Externally-funded faculty structures in Africa struggle with the same dependency.** A study of medical education departments at four sub-Saharan African medical schools supported by MEPI found that external and internal funding and educational innovations were key enablers, while challenges included *"establishing and maintaining the legitimacy of the department, staffing the departments with qualified individuals, and **navigating dependence on external funding**"* (Kiguli-Malwadde E et al. *BMC Med Educ.* 2015;15:109, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-015-0398-y)). According to PubMed. **[STALE]**.

A 2025 evaluation of a sub-Saharan African quantitative-methods faculty training programme (Dec 2017 – June 2020) built a network of 26 faculty members who went on to train 380 students across 8 countries (Botswana, Ethiopia, Ghana, Nigeria, Rwanda, South Africa, Tanzania, Uganda). Median increase in faculty knowledge: 17 percentage points (IQR 0, 20). **"The program relied on collaborative funding from participating institutions and focused on individual capacity-strengthening"** — an in-kind institutional model rather than a fee-for-service faculty model (Ntshebe O et al. *Glob Health Sci Pract.* 2025;13(1), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.9745/GHSP-D-22-00507)). According to PubMed.

### C4. Background attrition rate you are recruiting against

In a cross-sectional survey of 37,511 attending-level specialists at 15 academic medical institutions (Healthcare Professional Well-Being Academic Consortium; 18,719 respondents; data Oct 2019 – Jul 2021), **37.9% met criteria for burnout**, 39.3% for professional fulfilment, and **32.6% (5,177/15,890) reported at least moderate intention to leave their institution within two years**. Each 1-point increase in burnout was associated with an odds ratio of **1.52** for intention to leave. Protective factors: supportive leadership behaviours (OR 0.83), personal–organisational values alignment (OR 0.81), peer support (OR 0.93), perceived gratitude (OR 0.95) (Ligibel JA et al. *JAMA Netw Open.* 2023;6(12):e2347894, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.47894)). According to PubMed. **[STALE]** by data-collection window.

### C5. What this evidence supports (analysis, not new fact)

1. **Volunteer faculty models do survive** — Peoples-uni ran fifteen years and graduated 159 masters students on volunteers. What killed it was the absence of accreditation and a business model, both of which its founder flagged in year three.
2. **Expect to convert roughly half of expressions of interest into actual delivery** (Peoples-uni: ≤46% for development, 86% for delivery once someone had agreed to a specific slot). Recruit against the offer pool, not the required headcount.
3. **The single largest documented attrition risk is the first year of a faculty member's tenure** (Adams 2026), and the single largest documented barrier is protected time, not money (PRiMER 2025: 80%; CoPPRR: financial incentives "perhaps the least successful tool").
4. **Money does not recruit but its absence is named as a failure cause in LMIC settings** (South Sudan midwifery 2025). The reconciliation: honoraria appear to function as recognition and cost-recovery, not as market compensation. Michigan's implied USD 2.94–4.64/hour did not move behaviour; 67% still preferred to be paid.
5. **Named, credentialed, low-friction roles matter more than rates.** Credentialing friction was a top barrier for dermatology volunteer faculty; ICT friction was the stated cause of Peoples-uni's volunteer drop-off.
