# Non-Monetary Rewards and Recognition for Teaching Faculty

**Compiled:** 1 August 2026 | **All URLs accessed:** 1 August 2026

**[STALE]** = published before 2024, reported because it is the best available

## HEADLINE FINDING

The single most important result of this review is that **the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria explicitly credits teaching, examining and acting as a resource person toward the mandatory CPD that every Nigerian doctor must accumulate to renew their licence** — and that SQHN, as a professional society, can become an MDCN-recognised CPD provider for a ₦200,000 recognition fee plus ₦100,000 annual renewal, rather than the ₦1,000,000 visitation fee charged to fresh independent applicants. This converts faculty teaching time into a regulator-mandated currency the faculty member would otherwise have to buy, at a programme-level cost of roughly ₦300,000 in year one for the whole faculty body. Nothing else in this review has that cost-to-value ratio.

The second most important result is a caution: the best evidence (a 2024 BEME systematic review) finds clinical educators are motivated by **autonomous** reasons — enjoyment, connectedness, professional development, feeling valued — **over** incentives and rewards. A rewards catalogue is not a substitute for a well-run programme in which faculty feel competent, connected and genuinely valued. Design accordingly.

## (b) NARRATIVE: WHAT THE MOTIVATION EVIDENCE ACTUALLY SHOWS

### The strongest single source

**Orsini C, Imafuku R, Jennings B, Neufeld A, Tricio J, Kusurkar RA. "What influences clinical educators' motivation to teach? A BEME systematic review and framework synthesis based on self-determination theory: BEME Review No. 90." *Medical Teacher* 2024;47(5):779–787.** — **EVALUATED**, 29 studies (1998–2022), diverse disciplines and settings, analysed through SDT's amotivation / controlled / autonomous framework nested within motivation from "above" (stakeholders, societal expectation), "within" (personal beliefs), and "below" (perceived learner engagement).
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1080/0142159X.2024.2412166 | PubMed PMID 39402983 | Accessed 1 Aug 2026

The finding that should govern programme design, verbatim from the abstract:

> "Educators reported autonomous over controlled motivation to teach, favouring **enjoyment, connectedness, professional development, feeling valued for their teaching efforts, and altruistic reasons to teach, over being motivated by incentives and rewards**."

Read carefully, this is not an argument against a rewards package. It is an argument about **ordering**. The four things that carry the load — enjoyment, connectedness, development, feeling valued — are all things the programme can engineer at near-zero cash cost through how it runs, not through what it hands out. The rewards catalogue should be built to *signal and reinforce* those four, not to substitute for them.

### The practical translation

**Orsini C, Neufeld A. "Ten tips for supporting clinical teachers' motivation to teach: A self-determination theory perspective." *Canadian Family Physician* 2026;72:51–53.** — **DESCRIBED** (practice guidance grounded in cited evidence), and notably current.
https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026_OrsiniNeufeld_TenTips.pdf | Accessed 1 Aug 2026

Three tips bear directly on the director's question:

- **Tip 4 (affirm meaning, identity, purpose):** "Recognition is most effective when it reinforces meaning rather than offering token rewards." And: "Genuine student appreciation, personalised notes, or acknowledgment from leadership often carry more weight than generic institutional gifts." This is the single cheapest actionable finding in the review.
- **Tip 6 (invest in professional development):** "Opportunities for training, mentorship, peer observation, and reflection strengthen teachers' competence... When teachers feel they are improving and supported in doing so, their motivation shifts from **obligation toward aspiration**."
- **Tip 2 (involve teachers in shaping programmes and curricula):** "When teachers feel excluded from curricular decisions, stress and controlling teaching behaviour increases." For a programme with twelve coverage slots, giving faculty genuine input into their own module is a free autonomy-support lever.

### What faculty say they want, when asked directly

Two US surveys by the same group, both **EVALUATED**, both **[STALE]** but still the most direct preference data located:

**Kumar A, Loomba D, Rahangdale RY, Kallen DJ. "Rewards and incentives for nonsalaried clinical faculty who teach medical students." *J Gen Intern Med* 1999;14(6):370–2.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1525-1497.1999.00341.x | PMID 10354257
Survey of clerkship administrators at US and Canadian medical schools (338 responders). What programmes actually offered:

| Reward | % of programmes offering |
|---|---|
| Academic appointments | **90–95%** |
| Educational opportunities | **70–89%** |
| Appreciation letters | **74–84%** |
| Special recognition events | **62–79%** |
| Monetary payment | **13–22%** |

Only 3 of 338 respondents offered no rewards or incentives at all.

**Kumar A, Kallen DJ, Mathew T. "Volunteer faculty: what rewards or incentives do they prefer?" *Teach Learn Med* 2002;14(2):119–23.** DOI: https://doi.org/10.1207/S15328015TLM1402_09 | PMID 12058547
Survey of nonsalaried physician teachers in internal medicine, family medicine and paediatrics across the US. Two results matter:

1. **On the 1–5 appreciation rating**, the top item was travel and meeting registration reimbursement (**4.27**), with payment for teaching second (**3.94**). Educational opportunities rated highly "**especially when the school bore the cost** of providing a service."
2. **On forced rank-ordering (1–6)**, the picture inverts: **personal satisfaction ranked highest at 5.16**, while payment for teaching (**2.92**) and gifts or services from the college (**2.53**) came bottom.

The gap between (1) and (2) is the practically useful result. Asked to *rate* things in isolation, faculty appreciate almost anything offered. Asked to *rank* them against each other, meaning beats money and merchandise loses outright. A programme that cannot pay is not as disadvantaged as it fears — but a programme that substitutes branded tote bags for meaning is actively wasting money.

### The LMIC evidence base

There is no study of what motivates faculty to teach on a Nigerian, Ghanaian or Pakistani *professional/CPD* programme (see NOT FOUND #1). The adjacent evidence is nonetheless directionally strong and consistent.

**Willis-Shattuck M, Bidwell P, Thomas S, Wyness L, Blaauw D, Ditlopo P. "Motivation and retention of health workers in developing countries: a systematic review." *BMC Health Serv Res* 2008;8:247.** — **EVALUATED** [STALE]. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6963-8-247 | PMID 19055827
Twenty studies met inclusion. Seven motivational themes emerged: **financial rewards, career development, continuing education, hospital infrastructure, resource availability, hospital management, and recognition/appreciation.** Conclusion, verbatim: "financial incentives alone are not enough to motivate health workers. It is clear that **recognition is highly influential** in health worker motivation."

**Mathauer I, Imhoff I. "Health worker motivation in Africa: the role of non-financial incentives and human resource management tools." *Human Resources for Health* 2006;4:24.** — **EVALUATED** [STALE]. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1186/1478-4491-4-24 | PMID 16939644
Qualitative interviews in Benin (n=62) and Kenya (n=37) with doctors and nurses across public, private and NGO facilities. Findings relevant here:
- Over 50% cited **vocational calling and professional conscience** as their primary motivator
- **80% of Benin respondents ranked appreciation** as what they valued most
- **Nearly all respondents reported increased confidence after training**; two-thirds actively wanted further qualifications
- **80% wanted greater participation** in decisions affecting their work
- Verbatim: "Financial incentives are important... but increased salaries are by no means sufficient to solve the problem of low motivation."

A sharp warning inside this paper: health workers are "strongly guided by their professional conscience" yet become "demotivated and frustrated **precisely because they are unable to satisfy their professional conscience**" when supplies and management fail them. Applied to faculty: the fastest way to lose a good teacher is to invite them to teach and then run a shambolic session. Operational reliability *is* a motivational instrument.

**Olasoji HO, Mu'azu AB, Garba MH. "A study of clinical teachers' attitude to teaching and perceived learning needs in a medical college in Nigeria." *Adv Med Educ Pract* 2019;10:605–617.** — **EVALUATED** [STALE], and the only Nigeria-specific study located. DOI: https://doi.org/10.2147/AMEP.S171550 | PMID 31496862
Mixed methods, University of Maiduguri: 61 questionnaires (88% response) and 10 interviews.
- **Teachers derived satisfaction from teaching and maintained strong commitment** to it — the intrinsic base the programme can build on exists
- **80% reported no previous training in teaching**, yet **97% self-assessed their teaching as average or above**
- Most nonetheless said they needed to improve their teaching skill
- **No formal faculty development programme existed in the college**; skills were improved through "sporadic informal community of practice involving interested colleagues and modeling identified good teachers"

This is the commercial case for reward #2 in the table. Faculty development is scarce, wanted, and locally unavailable. The programme can be the first body to supply it. The 80%-untrained / 97%-self-confident gap also tells you *how* to sell it: not as remediation, but as an advanced credential and a community.

## (c) WHAT BACKFIRES — THE HONEST SECTION

### 1. Incentives can reduce motivation when they are impersonal, inequitable, inefficient or poorly framed

**Wisener KM, Driessen EW, Cuncic C, Hesse CL, Eva KW. "Incentives for clinical teachers: On why their complex influences should lead us to proceed with caution." *Medical Education* 2021;55(5):614–624.** — **EVALUATED**, interpretive description, 16 semi-structured interviews with clinical faculty. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.14422 | PMID 33222291

This is the most important cautionary source in the review. Verbatim findings:

> "Clinicians articulated interrelated and dynamic personal and environmental factors that had **linear, dual-edged and inverted U-shaped impacts** on their motivations towards teaching."

> "Clinical teachers were motivated when they felt valued and connected to their learners, peers, leadership, and/or the medical education community. While incentives aimed at producing these connections could be perceived as supportive, **they could also negatively impact motivation if they were impersonal, inequitable, inefficient, or poorly framed**."

> "Incentive schemes can be beneficial for teacher recruitment and retention, **but must be designed with nuance that takes into account what makes clinicians feel valued if the strategy is to do more good than harm**."

Four named failure modes — **impersonal, inequitable, inefficient, poorly framed** — should be used directly as a design checklist for the SQHN faculty reward scheme. Note especially *inequitable*: differentiating rewards between faculty who do comparable work is a documented harm mechanism, and a small faculty body of twelve makes any inequity immediately visible.

The paper also found that "barriers were frequently rationalised away, but **cumulative barriers often led to teaching attrition**." Faculty do not quit over one bad experience; they quit silently after the fourth administrative failure. Attrition will present as unavailability, not complaint.

### 2. Per diems and allowances — the LMIC-specific distortion

**Vian T, Miller C, Themba Z, Bukuluki P. "Perceptions of per diems in the health sector: evidence and implications." *Health Policy and Planning* 2013;28(3):237–246.** — **EVALUATED** [STALE]. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czs056 | https://academic.oup.com/heapol/article/28/3/237/551257

- **Scale of distortion:** in Uganda, low-level workers' nightly per diems (55,000 UGX) were "**close to 9 times the average daily wage**" (6,261 UGX). High-level officers received roughly twice their daily salary per night.
- **Attendance collapses without the allowance**, and is hollow with it: participants "would just attend the first session and then when they have a break they disappear."
- **Culture damage, verbatim:** "we have created a culture where people **expect to get something**... And that is not very healthy."
- **Work distortion:** "Work that involves writing... sometimes suffers because people want to go to the field."
- One genuinely two-sided finding worth holding: an employee said "I put more effort in my work because **an allowance shows that my work is recognised**." The allowance was functioning as a recognition signal. That is exactly the function a well-designed non-monetary instrument can take over — and the reason a *cheap but visible* reward can outperform a small cash payment.

**Scotland Malawi Partnership. "Practical Guidance and Support on Per Diems," v1.0, 5 September 2014.** — **DESCRIBED** (practitioner guidance). https://www.globalhealthpartnerships.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Practical-Per-Diem-Advice.pdf
Eight named harms, of which four are directly relevant: per diems undermine salaries; disrupt implementation because "once established, per diem discussions dominate project focus rather than outcomes"; create unfair competition where larger organisations outbid smaller local initiatives; and produce "**workshop syndrome**" — attendance driven by payment rather than development, with repeat attenders "giving false positive feedback" and thereby corrupting the programme's own evaluation data.
Its recommendations: address remuneration **at project start** ("prevention is better than cure"); reimburse **documented actual costs only**, with receipts; and **keep all rates equal regardless of role or rank** — which converges precisely with Wisener's *inequitable* failure mode.
**Honest limitation:** this document does not address certificates or non-monetary substitutes. It argues for removing financial incentives, not for replacing them with something else. It cannot be cited in support of the non-monetary package.

### 3. Awards are weak motivators and their reach is structurally limited

**Schindler N, Corcoran JC, Miller M, Wang C-H, Roggin K, Posner M, Fryer J, DaRosa DA. "Implementing an excellence in teaching recognition system: needs analysis and recommendations." *J Surg Educ* 2013;70(6):731–8.** — **EVALUATED** [STALE]. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsurg.2013.05.002 | PMID 24209649

> "There is **limited evidence** demonstrating effectiveness of existing teaching awards in medical education."

Leaders were moderately satisfied that awards *recognise* teaching and *demonstrate institutional value* — but "**less satisfied with awards for motivating faculty to participate in teaching or for contributing to promotion**." The structural reason is given plainly: "The ability of awards to affect outcomes such as participation in teaching and promotion may be **limited by the small number of recipients** for most existing awards. Most departments and institutions honor only a few recipients annually."

For a faculty body of ~12 plus coaches and assessors, a single annual award creates one winner and eleven non-winners. That is an inequity generator in a group small enough for everyone to notice. **Prefer universal, criterion-based recognition (every faculty member who meets a stated standard receives the same instrument) over competitive awards.**

### 4. Digital badges decay

**Noyes JA, Welch PM, Johnson JW, Carbonneau KJ. "A systematic review of digital badges in health care education." *Medical Education* 2020;54(7):600–615.** — **EVALUATED** [STALE], 30 papers. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.14060 | PMID 31971267

Verbatim: "**the novelty effect can negate the value of digital badges**." Study quality was "relatively moderate," and the review concludes there is "a **paucity of research** to support the design and implementation of this credentialing system in health care education."

**Womack-Adams K, Morbitzer KA, Ondek C, Collins H, McLaughlin JE. "A review of microcredentials in health professions continuing professional development." *Front Med* 2025;12:1532811.** — **EVALUATED**, current. DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2025.1532811 | PMID 39981092
Of 290 articles screened, **only 11 met inclusion**. "The definitions used for key terms like microcredential were **inconsistent across articles**." The evidence base is thin, not absent-but-promising.

Implication: badges are worth issuing as a *carrier* for something with independent value (a CPD credit, a verified faculty role) but are not themselves a motivator, and their pull declines as they proliferate.

### 5. Authorship as a reward is a live integrity risk

**ICMJE, "Defining the Role of Authors and Contributors."** — **DOCUMENTED**. https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html

All four criteria must be met — substantial contribution to conception/design or acquisition/analysis/interpretation; drafting or critical revision; final approval; **and** accountability for the whole work. ICMJE states: "All those designated as authors should meet all four criteria for authorship, and all who meet the four criteria should be identified as authors." Activities that alone do *not* qualify include "general supervision of a research group or general administrative support."

**Teaching on the programme does not, by itself, earn authorship on the programme's evaluation.** If the director offers co-authorship as a recruitment currency, the offer must be conditional on real contribution to the paper — otherwise the programme is systematically manufacturing gift authorship across its whole faculty, and doing so in writing. The safe construction is to offer *a route in*: faculty who join the evaluation working group, contribute to design or analysis, and revise the manuscript become authors; the rest are named in acknowledgements. That is defensible, still attractive, and costs nothing extra.

### 6. Two structural traps specific to this programme

- **The honorary title with no host.** Every honorary appointment source located is a *university* granting the title. SQHN is a professional society, not a degree-awarding institution. A title SQHN invents for itself ("SQHN Honorary Faculty") is a programme designation, not an academic appointment, and sophisticated mid-career clinicians will price it accordingly. To grant a title with real currency the programme needs a partner university's honorary appointments committee — which is a governance negotiation, not a cheap giveaway. Budget the relationship-building, not the certificate.
- **The Nigerian promotion mismatch.** Rewards #9 and #11 interact badly in Nigeria. At the University of Ibadan, teaching effectiveness is capped at 20 points while professorship requires 70 of 90 minimum points from publications, and the guidelines **do not explicitly recognise external or honorary teaching contributions** in the teaching-effectiveness score. For a Nigerian academic, therefore, hours spent teaching on an external programme are close to promotion-neutral, whereas a co-authored paper is directly convertible. If the programme wants to retain Nigerian university-based faculty, **the publication pathway is the load-bearing reward and the teaching title is decoration** — the inverse of the Ghanaian case, where KTU weights teaching at 55/100.

## PRACTICAL SYNTHESIS FOR THE DIRECTOR

**Do first, in this order:**

1. **Apply for MDCN CPD provider recognition as a specialty society/association** (₦20,000 + ₦200,000 + ₦100,000/yr). MDCN's published provider list confirms professional societies and associations are routinely recognised — the Nigerian Medical Association, Society of Family Physicians of Nigeria, Association of Public Health Physicians of Nigeria and others all appear. Accreditation is valid two years and renewable, with a stated maximum three-month processing time from application to licence grant. **Start now: a three-month clock against a January 2027 cohort leaves no slack, and the ₦1,000,000 visitation fee applies if the specialty-society route is not secured.**
2. **Write to NMCN, PCN and PMDC** on the three open credit questions (NOT FOUND #2, #3, #4). These determine whether nurses, midwives, pharmacists and Pakistani doctors can be paid in the same currency as Nigerian doctors — and therefore whether the faculty model is genuinely multiprofessional and genuinely exportable.
3. **Build the faculty development offer as the flagship reward**, not as an afterthought. It has the strongest evidence (BEME No. 90: professional development is a top autonomous motivator), the clearest local demand (80% of Nigerian clinical teachers untrained; no formal programme available), and marginal delivery cost.
4. **Institute universal, criterion-based recognition rather than competitive awards** — personalised acknowledgement from the director, learner testimony fed back by name, and a stated contribution record. Evidence supports personalised recognition (Orsini & Neufeld 2026) and is sceptical of awards (Schindler 2013). Cost: near zero.
5. **Design against Wisener's four failure modes.** Every proposed reward should be tested: is it *impersonal*? *inequitable* across the faculty? *inefficient* to claim? *poorly framed*? Any yes is a predicted motivation loss, not a neutral outcome.

**Do not:**
- Substitute branded gifts or merchandise for meaning (ranked last, 2.53, below even payment)
- Offer blanket co-authorship as a recruitment inducement (ICMJE breach; offer a genuine route in instead)
- Introduce per-diems or sitting allowances for faculty as a "small gesture" — the African per-diem literature shows this creates an expectation that is very hard to withdraw and corrupts both participation and evaluation data
- Build the Nigerian retention case on academic titles alone; at Ibadan, external teaching is close to promotion-neutral while publications carry ~78% of the professorship threshold
