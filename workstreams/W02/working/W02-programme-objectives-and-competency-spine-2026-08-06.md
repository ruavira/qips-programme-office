# Programme objectives and competency spine — DRAFT FOR THE COMMITTEE

**Revision 2, 6 August 2026.** Revision 1 reported three uncovered domains and treated them as
three gaps to accept. That was wrong, and the correction is visible below rather than quietly
applied: the three cost very different amounts, two of them are cheap or free, and one of those
two is the domain employers name most often. Coverage is now proposed at **seven of eight**, with
one deliberate exclusion.

**Status: a proposal, not canon.** Nothing here is decided. It is written to be argued with, and
the places where the programme office has no view are marked as such rather than filled in.

**What this document is.** Two things that turned out to be the same thing. Canon states that host
sites are qualified against a standard *"derived from programme objectives"* — a document
referenced in four places in this repository and existing in none of them. And the design has never
said what a graduate can do. Writing the objectives and writing the competency spine is one piece
of work, so it is one document.

Every comparison below is sourced in the accompanying evidence base.

---

## 1. The claim, in one paragraph

*Drafted by the programme office. This is the sentence everything else has to earn.*

> A graduate of this programme can take a quality or safety problem in their own service from a
> vague complaint to a measured, evidenced improvement that survives them — can read a service
> against a published standard and say where it falls short — and can teach a colleague to do
> both. They have done it once, under supervision, with twelve pieces of evidence to show for it
> and a run chart with real data in it. They are not a specialist in every domain of healthcare
> quality, and this programme does not claim they are.

Two clauses in that were put there by evidence rather than by preference.

*"Can teach a colleague"* — across eighteen real quality-role adverts, the most common duty asked
of a quality professional was not audit, not root cause analysis and not data. It was **training
and mentoring other staff**, in ten of the eighteen. The programme already builds toward this:
artefact 11 is a spread and handover case, and coaching capability is built internally rather than
bought in. It had simply never been stated as an outcome.

*"Read a service against a published standard"* — because accreditation systems are what employers
name when they name anything, and because this is the one domain the programme can add at almost no
cost. See section 3.

## 2. The twelve outcomes

Months 1 and 2 are **existing, committee-authorised text** reproduced verbatim from the curriculum
spine. Months 3 to 12 are **drafted here**, against artefact names the spine already carries.
Nothing below month 2 has been agreed by anyone.

| # | Artefact | The participant can… |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem definition and improvement opportunity | *(existing)* state a quality or safety problem in their own service in terms that are specific, bounded, evidenced and within their lawful influence — and say what they do not yet know |
| 2 | Process map and current-state analysis | *(existing)* map how the work actually happens — not how policy says it happens — and identify where the process fails the people it serves |
| 3 | Measurement plan and operational definitions | **draft** — define what will be counted, how, by whom and how often, in terms precise enough that two different people counting the same thing get the same number |
| 4 | Observership analysis | **draft, revised** — read a service other than their own against a published standard: say where it meets that standard, where it does not, what evidence would settle the difference, and what is transferable to their own setting and what is not |
| 5 | Driver diagram and cause analysis | **draft** — distinguish what is causing a problem from what is merely associated with it, and set out the chain from aim to primary driver to changeable thing |
| 6 | Change portfolio and prioritisation | **draft** — choose between candidate changes on stated criteria, and defend what they decided not to do |
| 7 | First test of change | **draft** — run a change at a scale small enough that being wrong is cheap, and record what actually happened rather than what was supposed to |
| 8 | Second test and adaptation | **draft** — adapt a change in response to evidence rather than to preference, and say what the first test taught them |
| 9 | Annotated run chart | **draft** — read variation correctly, distinguish signal from noise, and annotate a chart so that someone who was not there can see what changed and when |
| 10 | Sustainability and control plan | **draft** — say what will make the improvement decay, and what has been put in place so that it does not depend on them personally |
| 11 | Spread and handover case | **draft** — make the case for the change to a service that did not ask for it, and hand it to someone else in a form they can run |
| 12 | Capstone synthesis | **draft** — account for the whole arc to people who were not involved: what was attempted, what the evidence shows, what failed, and what they would do differently |

**Artefact 4 is where revision 2 changed something material.** The observership already sends a
participant into a site that has itself been qualified against the published Host Site Standard.
The instrument is already in the room. Asking them to read that service against a standard costs no
additional hours — it changes what the artefact asks for, not how long it takes — and it converts
a domain the design was going to exclude into one it demonstrates.

**Two features of the arc worth naming.** It moves through the stages the AAMC's competency set
spreads across a whole career — *describe and identify* (months 1–6), *demonstrate and model*
(7–9), *coach, lead and mentor* (10–12). Compressing that into a year is only honest because each
stage is anchored to a real artefact from the participant's own service rather than to an
examination. And month 4 is doing something no comparator does at all.

## 3. Coverage against the field's framework, and what each domain costs

NAHQ's eight domains are the yardstick, because they are what an informed buyer benchmarks
against. NAHQ's own position: *"All healthcare quality professionals should be at the Foundational
level in all 8 domains"* and *"It is not necessary to be an Advanced level in all areas."*

**The relevant budget.** Each month is 15.67 hours — eLearning 6.67, live session 3.0, coaching
1.0, artefact production 5.0 — giving 188 hours plus a 40-hour observership, which is the published
228. Of that, only the twelve eLearning releases are free content: 80 hours in total. The other
three components are tied to the participant's own project.

**And the eLearning releases are unwritten.** The spine specifies eLearning *hours* and nothing
else; months 3 to 12 are outline-only. So allocating one release to a subject is an allocation
decision, not a deletion. That is the fact that changes the arithmetic, and revision 1 missed it.

| NAHQ domain | Status | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Performance & Process Improvement | **Demonstrated, deep.** Nine of twelve artefacts. | Already built. This is the spine. |
| Health Data Analytics | **Demonstrated, bounded** — measurement, operational definitions, run charts. Not statistical process control beyond that, not population-scale analytics. | Already built. |
| Regulatory & Accreditation | **Taught and demonstrated** — via one eLearning release and the revised artefact 4. | **One of twelve unwritten releases, plus authoring effort.** No schedule change. SQHN is the accreditation body and the Host Site Standard already exists, so this is the cheapest domain for *this* programme specifically — and the one employers name. |
| Quality Leadership & Integration | **Partly demonstrated** — artefacts 11 and 12 require making a case to people who did not ask. | Already built. |
| Quality Review & Accountability | **Partly demonstrated** — artefact 10, the control plan. | Already built. |
| Professional Engagement | **Partly demonstrated, previously undeclared.** Artefacts 11 and 12 already do the "account to the profession" half. Ethics and professional-development planning are *not* covered and should not be claimed. | **Nothing.** A line in the capstone rubric. Note NAHQ's own exam does not test this domain either. |
| Patient Safety | **Taught, not demonstrated** — unless the participant's own project happens to be a safety project, in which case it is demonstrated by accident rather than by design. | Demonstrating it would mean constraining what problem a participant may bring. **This remains a real gap and the committee should see it as one.** |
| Population Health & Care Transitions | **THE SINGLE DELIBERATE EXCLUSION.** | Teaching it costs one to two releases and produces taught-but-not-practised coverage — the exact weakness that makes the comparator credentials thin. Demonstrating it would require the improvement project to cross a care boundary, which cannot be mandated. And no advert in the eighteen named it. |

**Seven of eight touched. Two demonstrated deeply, one newly demonstrated, three partly, one taught
only, one excluded on purpose.**

**The tier this supports, stated plainly.** Foundational across the seven, with demonstrated
evidenced practice in Performance & Process Improvement, in the bounded slice of Health Data
Analytics, and now in Regulatory & Accreditation. 228 hours cannot produce Proficient across eight
domains. Claiming otherwise would be a claim the design cannot carry and an informed buyer would
catch.

## 4. The trade this makes, stated so it can be rejected

Nine of the twelve artefacts sit in Performance and Process Improvement. That depth is the only
thing in the entire comparator set that nobody else offers at this price: ISQua's fellowship is a
fifth of the study hours with no supervised practice at all, and the two credentials are
examinations with no teaching attached.

Every hour moved into breadth is an hour moved onto ground where the competition is stronger.

Regulatory and Accreditation earns its place because SQHN makes it nearly free and employers ask
for it by name. Population Health would be buying breadth at the price of the thing that makes this
distinctive. That is the judgement, and it is the committee's to overturn.

## 5. What this programme does not claim

Written as plainly as the claims, because the programme's rules require it and because a
credential's non-claims are what make its claims believable.

- It does not make anyone a specialist in patient safety. The CPPS credential exists for that,
  costs USD 649 to a Nigerian candidate, and requires a degree plus three years.
- It does not prepare anyone for the CPHQ examination. CPHQ tests seven sections including
  Population Health and Care Transitions, which this programme deliberately does not teach.
- It does not confer accreditation-surveyor competence. Reading a service against a standard is not
  the same as being qualified to survey one, and the difference must not be blurred — least of all
  by the body that trains surveyors.
- It does not cover professional ethics or professional-development planning.
- It is not a substitute for a postgraduate degree, and the employer evidence is explicit that where
  Nigerian hospitals require quality-specific education at all, they require a degree.
- It does not claim institutional endorsement by ISQua. Faculty involvement is at individual level.

## 6. What an employer is buying — the case, and its weakness

*This section is the honest one. It does not read well, and it should not be softened.*

**The case.** A hospital gets back someone who has, over twelve months, defined a real problem in
that hospital, measured it, changed something, proved whether the change worked, made it survive
their own absence, and handed it to a colleague. The improvement stays in the building. They can
read the hospital against an accreditation standard and say where it falls short. And they can do
it again without us — which is the difference between buying training and buying capability.

**The weakness, and it is structural.** No Nigerian or Ghanaian regulator requires a hospital to
employ anyone qualified in quality. SQHN's own QI 5.1 is permissive — management *"may appoint"* an
individual — and mandates only a committee. Ghana's HeFRA sets degree floors for seven director
roles and has no quality post among them. There is no compliance driver in the launch market, so
the sale is entirely discretionary.

Worse, the labour market is barely visible: on 6 August 2026 the main Nigerian job boards carried
zero hospital clinical-quality roles between them. A participant cannot point their employer at a
job market that would reward the qualification, because it does not yet publicly exist.

**Which points at the same action the pricing work identified.** The highest-value move available
is not a marketing decision. It is SQHN amending QI 5.1 from *"may appoint"* to a scored criterion
with a qualification floor. It costs nothing, is entirely within SQHN's gift, and would create in
one stroke the demand this programme is otherwise trying to manufacture. Two independent pieces of
work have now arrived at it.

## 7. The strategic question this raised, which nobody asked for

There is no competency framework for healthcare quality professionals authored anywhere in Africa.
A 2026 scoping review in ISQua's own journal screened 14,144 records, included 118 articles, and
its table of publication regions does not contain Africa at all. A second 2026 review names
evaluation from an LMIC perspective as the field's priority gap.

**SQHN could author the first one.** It is the national accreditation body, it already publishes
facility standards, and it is about to run a programme that generates exactly the evidence such a
framework would need — forty improvement projects with baselines, in Nigerian hospitals, which
nobody currently holds.

That is a much larger proposition than a twelve-month programme, and it is not the programme
office's decision to take. But it would be a mistake to write these objectives without noticing
that the same document, done well, is the first draft of something considerably bigger.

## 8. What the committee is actually being asked

1. **Is the claim in section 1 the right claim** — and specifically, should *"can teach a colleague
   to do the same"* be an outcome the programme is accountable for, or an aspiration it should not
   promise?
2. **Is the single exclusion right?** Population Health and Care Transitions is left out on the
   argument in section 4. That argument can be rejected.
3. **Is one of the twelve eLearning releases well spent on Regulatory and Accreditation** — given
   that no release has been written yet, so this is an allocation rather than a cut?
4. **Is Patient Safety taught-but-not-demonstrated acceptable**, in a programme with patient safety
   in its name? This is the gap that survives revision 2.
5. **Should the tier be published?** Saying "Foundational" out loud is honest, and it also tells
   every buyer where the ceiling is.
6. **Does SQHN want to author the framework** — and if so, does cohort 1 become its evidence base?

Questions 1 to 5 are for the design. Question 6 is for SQHN, and it is above this programme.
