# Months 3 to 12 — spine-level artefact definitions — DRAFT FOR THE COMMITTEE

**Revision 1, 8 August 2026. A proposal, not canon. Nothing here is decided.**

**What this document is.** The director decided on 6 August that the next build is months 3 to 12
of the curriculum, spine level first. This document is that build: for each of the ten outlined
months, the artefact definition brought to the standard months 1 and 2 already meet at spine
level — the performance outcome, why the month sits where it does, the evidence the participant
submits, and how it connects to the capstone.

**What this document is not.** It is not the teaching content. The eLearning releases, learning
maps, live-session plans, artefact briefs, rubrics and accessibility equivalents for these ten
months remain unwritten, and Q014 — who authors them, on what schedule, against what review —
remains open and blocking. Reporting this document as "months 3 to 12 exist" would repeat the
failure Q014 records. It is also not an extension of the Item 1 prototype: the sitting of
1 August authorised months 1 and 2 in full and months 3 to 12 as outline, and this document does
not touch `engine/schemas/curriculum-spine.yaml`. It is written so that, if the committee adopts
it, the merge is mechanical — the definitions below are already in the spine's shape.

**What it is built from.** The twelve draft outcomes in
`W02-programme-objectives-and-competency-spine-2026-08-06.md` (section 2), extended to the full
four-field standard; the canon facts that fix the calendar — F003 and F004 (the monthly cycle),
F006 (observership month 3 to week 2 of month 4), F010 (measurement at month 3, run charts at
month 9), F019 (the 228-hour envelope, 5 hours of artefact production in an ordinary month); the
months 1–2 prototype; and the employer and competency evidence in
`W02-competency-evidence-base-2026-08-06.md`. No new external figures are introduced here; where
an evidence finding is cited, it is one already sourced in that evidence base.

**The arc these ten months complete.** Months 1–6 ask the participant to *describe and identify*;
months 7–9 to *demonstrate*; months 10–12 to *lead, hand over and account*. The claim drafted in
the objectives document promises three capabilities: take a problem in their own service to a
measured, evidenced improvement that survives them; read a service against a published standard;
teach a colleague to do both. Month 4 carries the second capability, month 11 the third, and the
other eight carry the first. The patient-safety gap — taught, not demonstrated — is not closed by
anything below, and ships visible, per the accepted default of 6 August.

## Month 3 — Measurement plan and operational definitions (A03)

### Performance outcome

The participant can define what will be counted, how, by whom and how often, in terms precise
enough that two different people counting the same thing get the same number — and can show that
the counting can actually be done in their service, with the people and records it already has.

### Why third

F010 places measurement at month 3, and the timing is arithmetic as much as pedagogy: the run
chart F010 places at month 9 needs points, and measures defined any later cannot supply six
months of them (UA5). The problem was defined in month 1 and located in a process in month 2;
counting before those two steps would count the wrong thing carefully. Month 3 also carries the
start of the 40-hour observership (F006), so this artefact deliberately leans on the participant's
own service records and existing data rather than new fieldwork — the design must not ask for
field observation in the month the participant is spending forty hours in someone else's service.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The measures that will show whether the improvement works — at least one outcome measure and
  one process measure — each with an operational definition: what counts, what does not, and the
  edge cases named.
- For each measure: who collects it, from what source, how often, and what the collection costs
  them in time.
- A baseline: existing data where it exists, or the first weeks of collection, with the source
  named either way.
- A statement of whether each measure is already collected in the service or must be newly
  collected — and, if new, where agreement stands with whoever will do the collecting.
- What could make the data misleading — gaming, seasonality, small numbers — stated before
  collection starts, not discovered after.

### Connects to capstone

The measures defined here are the ones the month 9 run chart plots and the capstone argues from.
A capstone without this month's definitions is assertion.

## Month 4 — Observership analysis (A04)

### Performance outcome

The participant can read a service other than their own against a published standard: say where
it meets the standard and where it does not, what evidence would settle the judgements forty
hours could not, and what transfers to their own setting and what does not — with the reason
either way.

### Why fourth

F006 fixes the observership at month 3 to week 2 of month 4, so the analysis lands here, while
the visit is fresh. The artefact is the participant's own analysis, not a log of attendance. Its
position directly before cause analysis is deliberate: a comparable service seen handling a
comparable process is the cheapest source of candidate changes the participant will get all year,
and those observations feed months 5 and 6. This is also the month that carries the
standard-reading capability: the host site has itself been qualified against the published Host
Site Standard, so the instrument is already in the room — the revision that put it here changed
what the artefact asks for, not how long it takes.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The standard used, named, and the parts of it the observership actually exercised.
- Where the host service meets the standard and where it does not — each judgement carrying the
  observed evidence for it, not an impression.
- The judgements forty hours could not settle, and what evidence would settle them.
- What transfers to the participant's own service and what does not, with the reason it does or
  does not.
- Nothing patient-identifiable, and nothing identifying host-site staff; judgements are framed
  for learning and shared within the observership agreement's confidentiality terms.

### Connects to capstone

Supplies the capstone's outside reference point: the candidate changes observed working elsewhere
enter the month 6 portfolio, and reading a service against a published standard is one of the
three capabilities the capstone claims.

## Month 5 — Driver diagram and cause analysis (A05)

### Performance outcome

The participant can distinguish what is causing a problem from what is merely associated with it,
and can set out the chain from aim through primary drivers to things that can actually be
changed — with the evidence for each link, and the links that rest on belief marked as belief.

### Why fifth

Cause analysis had to wait for the baseline. A driver diagram drawn in month 1 would have encoded
the team's opinions; drawn now, each link can be tested against the month 3 measures, the month 2
map and the month 4 observations. The month 1 discipline — say what you do not know — recurs
here as a structural requirement: unevidenced links are permitted, but only marked as such. The
month sits directly before the change portfolio because the changeable ends of these chains are
the portfolio's candidates.

### Evidence the participant submits

- A driver diagram from aim through primary drivers to changeable contributors.
- The evidence for each link — baseline data, the process map, observation, staff account — and
  which links rest on belief alone.
- Any cause the team held at month 1 that the evidence has since weakened or overturned, or a
  plain statement that none has.
- The two or three changeable contributors carried forward as candidates for month 6, and why
  those.

### Connects to capstone

This is the capstone's causal argument. The tests of months 7 and 8 test links in this diagram,
and the capstone stands or falls on whether the chain from aim to change survives them.

## Month 6 — Change portfolio and prioritisation (A06)

### Performance outcome

The participant can choose between candidate changes on stated criteria — impact, effort, risk,
and who has to agree — and can defend what they decided not to do.

### Why sixth

The halfway point, and the last month of describing before the doing begins. Everything before it
feeds candidates — the month 2 failure points, the month 4 transfers, the month 5 changeable
contributors — and nothing after it should introduce a change that never appeared here without
saying why. Selection is placed before testing so that month 7 tests a choice, not an impulse;
the set-aside list is written now because month 11 will be asked "what else did you consider?"
by a service that did not ask for the change.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The candidate changes, each traced to its source: a failure point, an observed transfer, or a
  driver.
- The criteria, stated before the scoring, and the scoring.
- The chosen first test and the reason it is first.
- What was set aside and why — including at least one attractive change rejected, with the
  reason it lost.
- Who must agree before the first test can run, and where that agreement stands.

### Connects to capstone

The capstone's answer to "why this change and not another". The set-aside list is what makes the
month 11 spread case credible.

## Month 7 — First test of change, recorded (A07)

### Performance outcome

The participant can run a change at a scale small enough that being wrong is cheap, and can
record what actually happened — including what did not go to plan — rather than what was supposed
to happen.

### Why seventh

The arc turns here from describing to demonstrating. A test this late is a design position: a
change tried without a baseline, a cause chain and a selection rationale is just trying things,
and the first six months are what make this test a test. Placing the first test at month 7 also
leaves two full test cycles before the month 9 run chart, so the chart has events to speak about.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The plan before the test: what was predicted, at what scale, over what period, measured by
  which of the month 3 measures.
- What actually happened, set against the prediction, in the measures' own numbers — small
  numbers included rather than dressed up.
- What did not go to plan, said plainly.
- The decision the test produced — adapt, hold at this scale, or abandon — with the reason.

### Connects to capstone

The first entry in the capstone's evidence of change. Prediction set against outcome is the
discipline the capstone's credibility rests on.

## Month 8 — Second test and adaptation (A08)

### Performance outcome

The participant can adapt a change in response to evidence rather than preference, can say what
the first test taught them, and can show that learning in the second test's design — not only in
its commentary.

### Why eighth

One test shows the participant can try; a second shows they can learn. The two sit in adjacent
months because a test cycle's lesson decays if it is not acted on, and because the run chart in
month 9 needs the second cycle's points. This is also where measurement continuity earns its
keep: the measures must run unbroken through both tests, or month 9 loses exactly the points that
carry the signal.

### Evidence the participant submits

- What the first test taught, stated as specific changes to the second test's design.
- The second test's plan and what actually happened, under the same discipline as month 7.
- The month 3 measures, continuing unbroken through both test months.
- Where the change now stands — working, adapted again, or abandoned. Abandonment with evidence
  is a legitimate outcome of the method, not a failure of the participant.

### Connects to capstone

The capstone's learning arc: what changed between the first test and the second is the
demonstration that method, not luck, produced the result.

## Month 9 — Annotated run chart (A09)

### Performance outcome

The participant can read variation correctly — distinguish signal from noise against stated
rules — and can annotate a chart so that someone who was not there can see what changed, when,
and with what result.

### Why ninth

F010 places run charts at month 9, and the timing is arithmetic: measures defined at month 3
and collected continuously have six months of points by now, roughly the minimum before run-chart
rules can say anything worth hearing (UA5) — and the chart arrives after two tests, so it has
events to annotate rather than a flat line to describe. Earlier, the chart cannot speak; later,
it cannot inform the sustainability plan that follows it.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The run chart, plotted from the month 3 measures, unbroken from baseline through both tests.
- Annotations tying movements to events: the tests, external shocks, data problems.
- The signal judgement — signal or no signal — made against named rules, not by eye.
- An honest account of any gap or break in the data, and what it does to the judgement.

### Connects to capstone

The capstone's centrepiece exhibit. The drafted claim promises "a run chart with real data in
it"; this is that chart.

## Month 10 — Sustainability and control plan (A10)

### Performance outcome

The participant can say what will make the improvement decay, and what has been put in place so
that it does not depend on them personally — named owners, continuing measures and trigger
points, not intentions.

### Why tenth

The arc turns from demonstrating to leading. Sustainability sits at month 10 rather than month 12
deliberately: a control plan written in the final month is a promise, while one written two
months out can be observed running — or failing — before the capstone reports on it. It follows
the run chart because a control needs a measure worth watching, and precedes the handover because
nobody should be handed work that has no plan for surviving them.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The decay risks, named: staff turnover, attention moving on, the old workaround returning.
- The control: which measure continues after the programme, who watches it, and what reading
  triggers what response.
- The named owner of the improvement after the participant steps back, and where that person's
  agreement stands.
- What the control costs the service in continuing effort, honestly stated.

### Connects to capstone

The capstone's claim to an improvement "that survives them" rests here — with two months of the
control plan actually running by the time the capstone is written.

## Month 11 — Spread and handover case (A11)

### Performance outcome

The participant can make the case for the change to a service that did not ask for it, and can
hand the work to someone else in a form that person can run — teaching it, not just documenting
it.

### Why eleventh

This is the teaching month. The employer evidence found training and mentoring other staff to be
the duty most often asked of a quality professional — ten of the eighteen adverts examined — and
the drafted claim promises a graduate who can teach a colleague. That has to be demonstrated
somewhere, and it is demonstrated here: late enough that the work being handed over is real and
evidenced, early enough that the capstone can report how the handover actually went rather than
that one is planned.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The spread case, addressed to a named service or team that did not ask for the change: what it
  is, the evidence it works, and what adopting it costs.
- The handover pack, written for the receiving person to run — their instructions, not the
  participant's notes.
- Evidence of one real teaching interaction: the colleague's own account, or a piece of work the
  colleague produced under the participant's guidance, shared with that colleague's consent.
- What was left out of the handover on purpose, and why.

### Connects to capstone

Demonstrates the third capability the capstone claims — teach a colleague — and produces the
witness the capstone can cite: someone else now runs the work.

## Month 12 — Capstone synthesis (A12)

### Performance outcome

The participant can account for the whole arc to people who were not involved: what was
attempted, what the evidence shows, what failed, what it cost, and what they would do
differently — argued from the eleven artefacts' own evidence, not from memory flattered in
retrospect.

### Why twelfth

The capstone is not a new piece of work; it is the argument the eleven prior artefacts already
support. It sits at month 12 because only now can it be assembled honestly: the control plan has
two months of running to report, the handover has landed or visibly not landed, and the run chart
has its closing points. A capstone month that demanded new work would break F019's envelope and
would invite exactly the retrospective gloss this design exists to prevent.

### Evidence the participant submits

- The synthesis: the problem (month 1), the case (months 2–6), the demonstration (months 7–9),
  the survival and spread (months 10–11), argued as one account for an audience that was not
  there.
- The run chart, final, carrying the closing months' points and the control period.
- What failed or fell short, given equal standing with what worked.
- A short professional close: what the participant will carry into their own practice, and what
  they would do differently — the capstone's line of Professional Engagement coverage, per the
  objectives document.

### Connects to capstone

This artefact is the capstone. Its connection is to the other eleven: it may introduce no claim
they do not evidence.

Month 3 carries a full monthly cycle plus the start of forty observership hours; A03 leans on existing records for exactly this reason, but no timing log has tested the combined load. | UNTESTED | F019's own notes already say the observership month is heavier; this asks whether it is too heavy. | W02 with W04 |

## The merge path, if the committee adopts these

**Nothing merges without a minute.** Three things happen together or not at all:

1. **The spine gains the definitions.** A YAML block in the spine's exact shape is held at the
   end of this document. The proposed detail level is `spine` — defined at spine level,
   prototype not authorised — so that the distinction Item 1 drew survives the merge: months 1–2
   `full`, months 3–12 `spine`, and nothing silently promoted.
2. **The validator's boundary moves with the minute, not before.** `engine/curriculum.py`
   currently refuses any month beyond 2 carrying `detail: full` — that check embodies Item 1 and
   must not be weakened by an agent. On adoption, it should refuse `full` beyond months 1–2
   exactly as now, and additionally require of every `spine` month the same three fields it
   already requires of `full` ones. The check should be proved to fire by restoring a defect —
   deleting one month's performance outcome — before the change is trusted.
3. **Q014 stays open.** These definitions answer none of Q014's actual question — who authors
   the ten months of teaching content, on what schedule, against what review. If anything, they
   sharpen it: the content now has a defined target to be authored against.

**What this unblocks meanwhile, even as a draft.** Q014's blocked list — W05, W06, W09, W10,
W11, W14 — needs artefact-level definition more than it needs finished teaching content: faculty
slot briefs can be drafted against named artefacts and outcomes, the coaching standard against
the evidence lists, the technology and calendar work against the cycle each month actually runs,
and marketing claims checked against outcomes that now exist in writing. None of that requires
this document to be approved first; it requires it to exist.

**What the committee is asked, plainly.**

1. Are these ten performance outcomes the right outcomes — and is each month's evidence list the
   right demand on five hours of production time in an ordinary month?
2. Is the `spine` detail level the right instrument for holding definitions the committee has
   adopted without authorising prototyping?
3. Does month 4's standard-reading artefact, as now defined, hold — given UA10?
4. Who authors the teaching content, by when, against what review — Q014, which this document
   deliberately does not answer and which is due 30 September.
