## Why this document exists

At Item 3 of the sitting the committee approved the faculty operating model but the dossier recorded that honorarium norms and total faculty cost could not be established. That left the tuition parameter (F024) and the viability arithmetic running without a faculty cost line, and it left the programme with no evidenced answer to the question of what it can offer faculty when it cannot pay them well.

Four parallel research streams were run to close that gap: faculty compensation norms, non-monetary faculty reward, host-site and in-kind value exchange, and self-sustainability mechanics. This document is the synthesis and the recommendation that follows from it.

Every figure below carries a live URL and an accessed date in the source dossiers, which are held at `workstreams/W05/working/` and `workstreams/W16/working/`. Where evidence could not be found, this document says so rather than filling the gap.

## 1. The single most important finding

**The Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria credits teaching, examining and acting as a resource person toward the mandatory CPD that every Nigerian doctor must accumulate to renew a licence.**

MDCN Category 6 — "Miscellaneous (Accreditors, Examiners, Resource persons)" — credits resource persons at accredited programmes. The requirement is 20 credit units a year, 40 per biennial licence renewal, one unit per contact hour, drawn from at least three of seven activity groups.

SQHN can become an MDCN-recognised CPD provider at the specialty-society and association rate: **₦20,000 application, ₦200,000 recognition, ₦100,000 annual renewal** — rather than the ₦1,000,000 visitation fee charged to fresh independent applicants.

That is roughly **₦300,000 in year one for the entire faculty body**, with zero marginal cost per faculty member thereafter. In exchange, every hour a faculty member teaches on the programme becomes an hour of the regulator-mandated CPD they would otherwise have to buy by attending a conference.

Nothing else in this research has that cost-to-value ratio. It converts the faculty cost line from a cash expense into a one-off registration fee.

Ghana's Medical and Dental Council does the same thing more narrowly: a resource person at an accredited event earns 1 credit per paper *in addition to* the programme credit, against a requirement of 15 points a year from at least three events. The terms for Ghana MDC event accreditation could not be established and are recorded as an open question.

**What is not yet established:** whether the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria and the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria credit teaching in the same way. NMCN's provider accreditation terms are published and are materially more expensive — ₦50,000 application, ₦250,000 on approval, ₦100,000 triennial renewal, **plus 5% of total income remitted to Council**, plus a requirement that at least 30% of the provider's board be NMCN-licensed nurses or midwives. That 5% revenue share is a real commercial term and needs to be modelled before nursing CPD recognition is pursued. PCN's activity-level credit rules were not published on accessible pages.

## 2. What the evidence says about paying faculty

### Money is a weak lever, and this is measured, not asserted

The definitive review is **BEME Review No. 90** (Orsini et al., *Medical Teacher* 2024, 29 studies, 1998–2022), which analysed clinical educators' motivation through self-determination theory. Its finding, verbatim:

> "Educators reported autonomous over controlled motivation to teach, favouring enjoyment, connectedness, professional development, feeling valued for their teaching efforts, and altruistic reasons to teach, over being motivated by incentives and rewards."

Three independent datasets corroborate this from different directions.

**Michigan's natural experiment.** The MDHHS Clinical Preceptor Incentive Pilot paid USD 500 to USD 1,500 per preceptor for 120 to 323 hours committed. Of 274 outreach attempts, 60 accepted, 43 declined and 154 did not respond — a 21% conversion. The finding that matters: **88% of respondents reported the financial incentive had not influenced or had only slight influence on their participation, while nearly 67% said they would prefer monetary compensation.** People want to be paid and are not moved by being paid. The implied hourly rate was USD 2.94 to USD 4.64 — a token, and it behaved like one.

**What faculty say when forced to rank.** In a US survey of non-salaried physician teachers, on a simple 1–5 appreciation rating the top item was travel and registration reimbursement (4.27) with payment second (3.94). But on forced rank-ordering the picture inverts completely: personal satisfaction ranks highest at 5.16, payment for teaching falls to 2.92, and gifts or branded merchandise come dead last at 2.53. Asked what they like, faculty say money. Asked what actually moves them, they say meaning.

**Time, not money, is the binding constraint.** In a survey of 228 family physicians, financial compensation ranked *last* as an initial motivator at 2.9%; 80% identified "dedicated time for teaching without impacting productivity" as necessary to continue; and **41.5% of current preceptors had already considered discontinuing clinical teaching.**

### But unpaid does not mean free

Roughly half to four-fifths of community-based health professions faculty in the United States are unpaid — 81% in one seven-centre study, and only 50% of North Carolina preceptors reported receiving any compensation in a January 2025 survey. So a substantially volunteer faculty is normal, not exotic.

The cautionary case is **Peoples-uni**, the closest structural analogue that exists: fifteen years of operation, volunteer tutors from 55 countries, 70% of reach in Africa, 159 master's graduates — and it **closed in March 2021**.

Read the closure carefully, because the obvious lesson is the wrong one. The volunteer model held for fifteen years. What gave way was accreditation: the founder named it as an unresolved challenge in 2009, the independent 2018 evaluation still named it, and the Manchester Metropolitan partnership that carried the award ended. **The volunteers survived; the un-accredited institution around them did not.**

Two volunteer-conversion numbers from that programme are worth carrying into planning. Of "more than 100" people who volunteered to develop content, 46 actually contributed — **at most 46% conversion from expression of interest to work done**. Of 29 who agreed to facilitate delivery, 25 were active — 86%, but against a role the paper describes as "very limited time commitment."

**Plan to recruit roughly twice the faculty you need.**

### Where the attrition actually is

Preceptor retention in a well-run programme runs at about **84.5% a year**. The only statistically significant negative predictor of retention in a study of 965 preceptors was **being a first-year preceptor** (p = 0.006). Specialty, faculty development participation and assessment-form difficulty were all non-significant.

Separately, 58% of preceptors in a 2024 national survey believed precepting contributes to burnout, and departures were driven by personal and structural factors — needing a break (30.1%), leaving clinical practice (20.1%), decreased clinical time (19.3%) — rather than dissatisfaction with the curriculum.

**The design implication is specific: put the support into the first year of a faculty member's tenure, not into the reward at the end of it.** And expect attrition to present as quiet unavailability rather than as complaint — the same literature finds that "barriers were frequently rationalised away, but cumulative barriers often led to teaching attrition." Faculty do not quit after one bad experience. They disappear after the fourth administrative failure.

## 3. The non-monetary reward package, ranked by evidence

These are ordered by what the evidence supports, not by what sounds generous.

**Tier 1 — strong evidence, near-zero cost, high value here**

1. **MDCN CPD credit for teaching, examining and resource-person roles.** Cost: ~₦300,000 for the whole faculty in year one. Value: the currency the regulator forces every faculty member to buy.
2. **Faculty development as the reward itself.** A Nigerian study found **80% of clinical teachers had no previous training in teaching** and their institution had no faculty development programme at all. Training people to teach is both the cheapest reward available and the thing that most improves the product. The BEME review found professional development among the strongest autonomous motivators.
3. **Personalised, meaning-linked recognition.** Not certificates and mugs — named acknowledgement from leadership, and learner testimony fed back to the faculty member. The current guidance is explicit: "Genuine student appreciation, personalised notes, or acknowledgment from leadership often carry more weight than generic institutional gifts." In a systematic review of LMIC health-worker motivation, appreciation ranked as the top motivator for 80% of Beninese health workers.
4. **A longitudinal faculty role and a real community of practice**, rather than twelve disconnected speaking slots. Connectedness was among the strongest autonomous motivators in the BEME review, and Nigerian clinical teachers describe already improving through a "sporadic informal community of practice" that they want formalised.

**Tier 2 — good evidence or strong documented practice, modest cost**

5. **Sponsored ISQua Fellowship or membership for core faculty.** €225 first year at the lower-middle-income rate, €150 renewal, with 30% off for institutional enrolments of ten or more. An internationally portable post-nominal, journal access and conference discounts, for roughly USD 250 a head.
6. **Reciprocal access** — free or marginal-cost seats on the programme's own content and future cohorts. Faculty rate educational opportunities highly, "especially when the school bore the cost."
7. **Honorary, adjunct or visiting faculty title.** Near-universal practice (90–95% of US clerkship programmes offer academic appointments) and zero cash cost — Edinburgh's honorary titles carry "no monetary compensation." The cost is governance: eligibility criteria, a committee and periodic review. Note the honest caveat: a 2024 US study found appointments drew "mixed appraisal."
8. **Career-progression linkage, but only where it works.** This is country-dependent and the difference is stark. Koforidua Technical University in **Ghana** weights teaching at **55 of 100 promotion points**. The University of Ibadan in **Nigeria** caps teaching effectiveness at **20 points**, while professorship requires roughly 78% of the minimum points from publications. **Teaching does not advance a Nigerian academic career; publishing does.** Which is why the next item matters more in Nigeria than anywhere else.
9. **Co-authorship on programme evaluation and improvement publications** — earned against all four ICMJE criteria, never gifted. BMJ Open Quality charges £1,000 for a Quality Improvement Report, with full waivers available for Hinari Core Offer countries. In a market where promotion is publication-driven and teaching is not, a genuine authorship pathway is worth more than an honorarium.

**Tier 3 — weak evidence, use sparingly or not at all**

10. Certificates and letters of appreciation. Widely offered (74–84% of programmes), but the value is in the personalisation, not the artefact.
11. Digital badges. "The novelty effect can negate the value of digital badges." Credly has no free tier. Self-issued Open Badges are near-free if wanted.
12. **Named "Teacher of the Year" awards — recommend against.** "There is limited evidence demonstrating effectiveness of existing teaching awards," and leaders were specifically *less* satisfied with awards for motivating participation. The structural problem is decisive at this scale: **a single annual award in a faculty of twelve creates one winner and eleven non-winners.** Prefer universal, criterion-based recognition where everyone meeting a stated standard receives the same instrument.
13. **Gifts and branded merchandise — recommend against.** Ranked dead last of all rewards tested, below cash.

## 4. What backfires

This section exists because a reward scheme designed without it does harm.

**The four named failure modes.** A 2021 interpretive study of clinical faculty found incentives had "linear, dual-edged and inverted U-shaped impacts" on motivation, and that incentives "could also negatively impact motivation if they were **impersonal, inequitable, inefficient, or poorly framed**." Those four words should be the design checklist.

**Inequity is the acute risk here.** A faculty body of twelve is small enough that every difference in treatment is visible to everyone. Differentiating rewards between faculty doing comparable work is a documented harm mechanism.

**The per-diem trap.** In Uganda a nightly per diem for a junior health worker was close to **nine times** the average daily wage. Tanzania spent USD 390 million on allowances in 2009 — 59% of annual salary spending. The behavioural consequence, in participants' own words: "If there is a workshop whereby people will receive no allowances, most people would not attend." And: "we have created a culture where people expect to get something... that is not very healthy." Practitioner guidance names "workshop syndrome" — attendance driven by payment, repeat attenders giving false positive feedback, and the programme's own evaluation data corrupted as a result.

**The recommendation that follows: the programme never pays participants to attend, and never pays faculty a per-diem-style allowance for virtual sessions.** Reimburse documented actual costs against receipts, at rates equal regardless of role or rank. Address remuneration at the start, in writing, because "once established, per diem discussions dominate project focus rather than outcomes."

One genuinely two-sided finding worth holding: a worker in the per-diem study said "I put more effort in my work because an allowance shows that my work is recognised." The allowance was functioning as a *recognition signal*. That is precisely the function a cheap but visible non-monetary instrument can take over — and the reason recognition can outperform a small cash payment rather than merely substituting for it.

## 5. Host sites and the observership

### The finding that inverts the assumption

**Observerships are frequently a revenue line for the host, not a cost.** Barts Health charges **£1,600 for a four-week observership**. Reliance Foundation Hospital Mumbai charges **INR 1,000–3,000 per week** for national observers and **USD 75–150 per week** for international ones. Children's Hospital of Philadelphia charges a **USD 750** administrative fee.

So asking a host site to take QIPS participants for nothing is not asking them to absorb a cost they would otherwise avoid. It is asking them to forgo revenue they could charge. The value exchange has to be worth at least that.

### The strongest lever, and it is not what was expected

**Accreditation credit for *hosting learners*: NOT FOUND** in any of the five standards sets examined — SQHN's own, Punjab Healthcare Commission, KP and Sindh Healthcare Commissions, COHSASA or JCI. That lever does not exist and should not be claimed.

- JCI QPS.01.00 requires that "a qualified individual(s) guides" the quality programme.
- Punjab Healthcare Commission Indicator 95 requires a designated quality-improvement individual with a job description "defining requisite qualifications."
- Sindh Healthcare Commission 4.5.16–4.5.18 carries equivalent requirements.

### The in-kind menu

The Victorian government publishes a schedule of maximum chargeable placement fees with an appendix titled "Suggested options for in-kind arrangements" listing roughly 25 named non-cash items that can substitute for payment. That is a government-published barter template and it is directly adaptable to the Host Site Standard.

### Indemnity — the number is real and large

Queensland Health's student placement deed requires the education provider to carry **AUD 10 million public liability per occurrence** and **AUD 10 million per claim / AUD 20 million aggregate professional indemnity**.

Non-clinical observer status materially reduces but does not eliminate the question, and jurisdictions split on it. London Health Sciences Centre states observers are explicitly **not** covered by hospital liability insurance. Barts Health's honorary contract **does** confer NHS indemnity. The GMC notes NHS indemnity does not extend to non-NHS bodies.

**This directly informs open question Q001.** The observership must be documented as strictly non-participatory, and the indemnity position must be settled per jurisdiction before host-site MoUs are signed — which is also the declared decide-by for parameter F007.

## 6. The Industrial Training Fund — three corrections to the record

Prior QIPS work recorded the ITF as a 1% payroll levy on employers with **25 or more employees**, reimbursable at up to **60%**. All three elements need correcting.

**Correction 1 — the threshold.** The ITF (Amendment) Act 2011, signed 3 June 2011 and gazetted 22 June 2011, reduced the threshold from 25 employees to **"a minimum of 5 employees or a turnover of ₦50 million and above per annum."** This makes the addressable employer base dramatically larger than previously assumed — most private hospitals of any size are in scope.

**Correction 2 — the refund rate.** The same amendment reduced the refund from 60% to **50%**, "subject to the training programme of the employer being in accordance with the Fund's reimbursement schemes."

**Correction 3 — it is not an entitlement.** ITF TR Form 1A, retrieved directly from itf.gov.ng, carries columns for **"Max % Claimable"** and **"% Awarded"** by training area. Reimbursement is rate-capped per training area and discretionary, running through a multi-stage approval — area office, Finance and Strategy Division, audit certification, payment authorisation. The 50% is an outer cap, not a rate.

**The process, which has hard dates:** annual training plan submitted before **31 March**; course approval obtained from ITF **in advance** — two weeks ahead for local training, three for international; claims filed on TR Forms 1 and 2 by **30 June** after the training year. The 30 June deadline is confirmed verbatim on the primary form.

**What could not be established.** The ITF website's narrative content is JavaScript-rendered and returns nothing to a non-JS client; only three official forms were retrievable. And the Nigeria Tax Act 2025's 4% Development Levy consolidates the Tertiary Education Tax, IT levy, NASENI levy and Police Trust Fund levy — **the ITF levy is not named in any consolidation list found.** The reasonable reading is that it survives, but **that is inference from silence and must be confirmed directly with ITF or FIRS before it appears in any marketing or sales conversation.**

**Assessment.** If it holds, the ITF reframes the institutional sale entirely: a Nigerian private hospital with five or more employees is already paying this levy and mostly not claiming it. The pitch is not "new spend" but "recovering money you have already paid." That is a materially easier conversation, and it needs primary verification before anyone has it.

## 7. What it costs to run this

### The best available cost anchor, and its limits

A costing study published January 2025 (2021 USD, n=89) priced a blended antenatal care training course across Kenya, Tanzania and Nigeria:

| Site | Blended, total cost per participant | Face-to-face comparator |
|---|---|---|
| **Nigeria (Oyo)** | **USD 860** | USD 909 (35% higher) |
| Kenya (Vihiga) | USD 860 | USD 1,144 (83% higher) |
| Tanzania (Dodoma) | USD 830 | — |

One-off design cost of the blended package: **USD 21,261**.

**Do not read USD 860 as a 12-month unit cost.** That programme was roughly a five-day-equivalent intervention. What transfers is the cost *architecture*:

- **The face-to-face component was 67–87% of total implementation cost.**
- Allowances, per diems and accommodation were **30–40%** of implementer direct cost.
- Labour 23–25%. Transportation 11–24%. Technology 11%.

**The controlling conclusion: in-person days are the entire variable cost story.** Every in-person day removed drops close to fully to contribution margin. Every additional geography served adds cost superlinearly. The 40-hour observership is therefore the single largest lever in the cost model — which argues strongly for the F007 parameter to settle at "own city or own country" rather than centralised sites.

### Platform costs are negligible

| Platform | Plan | Price |
|---|---|---|
| MoodleCloud Mini | 100 users, 2.5 GB | **USD 270 per year** |
| MoodleCloud Small | 200 users, 5 GB | USD 500 per year |
| Teachable Builder | 1,000 students, **0% transaction fee** | USD 69/month billed annually |
| Thinkific Start | 10,000 students | USD 82/month annually + 2.9% |

Teachable's Starter tier carries a **7.5% transaction fee** and a 100-student cap — the wrong tier for a fee-charging programme. Builder at USD 69/month with no transaction fee is the first commercially sane option. Canvas and Docebo publish no list pricing.

**Platform is not a meaningful cost. Faculty, travel and allowances are.**

### The accreditation cost, if pursued

ISQua's External Evaluation Association accredits *training programmes* on a four-year award with an **annual** fee by World Bank income band. For a lower-middle-income country: **€3,952 in 2026, €4,071 in 2027**, plus a one-time access fee for new clients whose amount is not published, with a 5% discount if the organisation holds another ISQua accreditation.

That is roughly USD 4,500 a year, every year, across a four-year cycle. It is the most significant single recurring cost line identified — and, given the Peoples-uni lesson, arguably the most important one.

## 8. Break-even, cohort size and completion

### Break-even is not the constraint

The only published break-even analysis for health professional education (Australia, RCT-based, n=85) found break-even at **7 participants** for web-based delivery against an AUD 250 fee, with fixed cost AUD 1,590 and variable cost AUD 8 per participant. The comparable face-to-face break-even was 14–20.

The only published institutional viability rule found is the University of Southampton's: **15 students on a rolling three-year average**, with an explicit three-year establishment period before the rule bites.

A cohort of 25 to 64 clears both comfortably on the fixed-cost test. **The risk is not in the fixed block; it is in the allowance-and-travel-heavy variable block.**

### Cohort size should be a multiple of eight

Coaching runs in groups of about eight. Each group is a **stepped cost**, not a smooth variable one. That makes the economically clean cohort sizes 24, 32, 40, 48, 56 and 64 — anything in between pays for a coaching group it does not fill.

**Recommendation for F025:** keep the declared range 25–64 but add a target of **40 (five coaching groups)** for cohort 1, with **24 (three groups)** as the viability floor and **64 (eight groups)** as the delivery ceiling.

### Completion is the number most likely to disappoint

The best LMIC anchor found is a Guinea study of two three-month blended courses (n=282, Moodle plus face-to-face workshop): **69.5% completion, 22% dropout, 9% abstention**, with success among completers at 80% — giving **overall success of 55%**.

**A twelve-month programme is four times longer. The honest planning position is that 69.5% is an optimistic ceiling, not a central estimate.** No completion figure for a 12-month blended professional programme in an LMIC was found in any source.

Two findings sharpen this:

**Attrition is stratified by profession, severely.** In the Guinea data, doctors succeeded at 53–67% on the same course where **nurses succeeded at 9%**. For a deliberately multi-professional cohort, headline completion will be driven as much by cohort composition as by programme quality. The nursing and allied cohort will need materially more support than the medical one, and this should be designed in rather than discovered in month six.

**The binding constraint is not what you would guess.** An Ethiopian multi-centre study (n=316, published 2026) found e-learning utilisation predicted by **workload, institutional ICT infrastructure and technical support** — while digital literacy and internet quality were **not** independently predictive. That points retention investment at employer engagement and institutional support, not at learner-facing technology training.

### On instalments

**Direct evidence linking payment structure to completion: NOT FOUND.**

What exists is the payment-depreciation literature — a consumer will "gradually adapt to a historic cost with the passage of time, thereby decreasing its sunk-cost impact" — which implies that payments made close to the consumption occasion pull harder on attendance than payments made long before. Applied to a twelve-month programme, that favours instalments timed to module starts over a single January payment that has psychologically depreciated by June.

**This is a theoretical extrapolation from a 1998 consumer-behaviour experiment. It is not evidence about professional programme completion and should not be presented to the committee as evidence.** The countervailing risk — that instalments create collection exposure and permit mid-year walk-away — is not addressed by any source found.

The comparators do use instalments: ACHS QIL offers AUD 4,163.50 in full or five monthly payments of AUD 693.92; an Indian six-month certificate offers INR 45,000 or three instalments of INR 15,000. Neither publishes completion by payment mode.

## 9. Recurring revenue — the only leg that compounds

**Credential maintenance beats alumni dues, and the evidence is one-sided.**

The alumni-dues model is in structural decline in its home market: **17% of alumni organisations operated a dues-paying programme in 2024, down from 26% in 2016**, with 80% now on non-dues models, average dues-paying membership halved from 62,719 to 31,686, and 71% of dues programmes reporting flat or declining membership.

The credential-maintenance annuity is the better-evidenced going concern:

| Credential | Maintenance rule | Fee |
|---|---|---|
| **ISQua FISQua** | Annual renewal, 40 credits a year after year one | **LMIC €150/year** (LIC €100, UMIC €225, HIC €300), 30% off for 10+ institutional |
| **NAHQ CPHQ** | Two-year cycle, 30 CE credits | Non-member USD 273–343; standard member USD 185–255; bundled member USD 0 |

Association renewal benchmarks: **84% median steady-state renewal**; first-year renewal somewhere in a **57–74% band** (two secondary reports of the same publisher's series are irreconcilable and neither primary report was accessible — treat 84% as the better-supported planning number and first-year renewal as uncertain).

**The configuration the evidence supports: a dues-free alumni community with a paid credential-maintenance obligation layered on top.** Not an alumni subscription.

**Note the interaction with the Item 2 decision.** The committee approved a completion certificate, not a certification. A completion certificate does not naturally carry a maintenance obligation — you cannot un-complete something. If the programme wants the annuity, the maintenance has to attach to something else: a register of practising graduates, a community membership with a standard, or the eventual move to Option C certification. **This should be designed now even though it cannot be sold until later**, because retrofitting a maintenance obligation onto graduates who were promised a one-off certificate is a broken promise.

## 10. Licensing as a second leg — be sceptical

**The global benchmark for replication is free.** Project ECHO, the most successfully scaled health-education replication model in the world, charges **no fee** to become a replication partner. It monetises through grants and service contracts, requiring only a Statement of Collaboration, an IP terms agreement and mandatory partner launch training.

Where licensing fees are published, they are modest. The University of Minnesota licenses up to 35 hours of global health content at **USD 5,000 initial / USD 2,500 renewal** hosted on its own server, or USD 4,000 / USD 2,000 on the licensee's LMS — note the clean **50% renewal ratio**, which is the useful structural precedent.

The UK government's own study of transnational education found an average annual fee of **£1,530 per active student** across all modes, and stated that franchise and validation arrangements yield "much lower" per-student fees — while explicitly conceding it could not calculate net value because institutions do not disclose costs.

**Licensing is a volume business with a low per-student margin. It is not a route to break-even for a 25–64 person first cohort, and any business case that assumes licensing fees will materialise from African institutions should be tested against Project ECHO's zero-fee benchmark.**

**The better second leg may be the inverse.** ISQua's EEA fee schedule — income-banded, annual, four-year-locked, with a cross-product discount — is simultaneously a cost line for SQHN *and* the exact commercial template for how an accrediting body monetises accrediting other people's training programmes. **If SQHN's second revenue leg is accrediting West African training providers rather than licensing its own curriculum, this is the published model to copy.** That plays to an asset SQHN already has and Ruavira does not: standing as an accreditor.

**One clean warning on train-the-trainer.** A cluster randomised trial found train-the-trainer at USD 1,002 per cohort against train-the-trainer-plus-expert-consultation at USD 1,431 — **43% more expensive, and outcomes were worse, not better.** The marginal expert-hour is the expensive, low-yield ingredient. Whether the cascade genuinely lowers unit cost at scale or merely relocates it onto downstream institutions remains unestablished.

## 11. THE PRICE RECOMMENDATION

### The convergence

Three markets, three currencies, three salary systems, three regulators — and the committed self-pay bands converge:

| | Realistic committed self-pay | Hard ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| **Nigeria** | USD 294–661 (₦400k–₦900k) | ~USD 600 (WACP Fellowship exam parity, ₦812,500) |
| **Ghana** | USD 530–970 (GHS 6,000–11,000) | USD 1,015 (60% of the University of Ghana MPH) |
| **Pakistan** | USD 270–540 (PKR 75k–150k) | ~USD 540 |

They converge because in each case the number is anchored to the same thing: **one specialist examination fee.**

**There is exactly one number inside all three bands and under all three ceilings: USD 500.**

### The recommended ladder

Published openly, on the price page, anchored to World Bank income bands — the ISQua pattern, which is the clearest precedent in this sector and the only one that publishes rather than buries its ladder.

| Rung | Price | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual, lower-middle-income** (Nigeria, Ghana, Pakistan) | **USD 500** | The single point inside all three committed bands and under all three ceilings. 84% of the WACP Fellowship exam fee — defensibly "less than a fellowship exam" |
| **Institutional / employer-sponsored, per seat** | **USD 900** | Inside Nigeria's evidenced institutional band; rides an existing budget line rather than creating one |
| — 3+ seats | USD 765 (−15%) | Matches the observed industry band exactly: IHI discounts 15% at 3 seats |
| — 10+ seats | USD 675 (−25%) | Within the observed 19–30% band at 10 seats (IHI 19%, ISQua 30%) |
| **International (upper-middle and high income)** | **USD 1,500** | 3× the LMIC rate, matching ISQua's published LIC:HIC ratio of 1:3 |
| **Hardship / low-income country** | **USD 250** | 50% of the LMIC rate, a capped number of places, funded by cross-subsidy — the ISQua LIC pattern |

### Payment structure — this is the product, not a concession

**USD 500 payable as four quarterly instalments of USD 125**, or twelve monthly instalments of USD 42, with **progression gated on payment**.

Three independent constraints force this:

1. **GTBank caps naira-card international transactions at USD 1,000 per quarter** (USD 500 ATM sub-limit). A USD 125 quarterly instalment fits comfortably; a single USD 500 payment is possible but tight for a retail customer without a domiciliary account.
2. **Ghana's average mobile money transaction is GH¢372 (~USD 33)** across GH¢3.01 trillion of 2024 volume. Mobile money is a high-frequency, low-ticket rail. A single large payment sits outside its normal envelope and will hit wallet limits. Paystack Ghana settles to a MoMo wallet with no minimum.
3. **Progression gating is the only enforceable leverage available.** The ALX structure — access to the next term as the lever — works. A legal claim on future income does not, which is the Decagon lesson.

**Price in USD, publish three local prices, revise per cohort.** The West African College of Physicians already does exactly this — USD 625 / ₦812,500 / GHC 8,125 for the same exam. It is the template, it is from this market, and it is the strongest single artefact in the whole pricing evidence base.

### Why not lower, and why not higher

**Not lower than USD 500.** Below roughly USD 400 the programme sits at IHI Open School subscription level (USD 399 for 38 self-paced courses) and the **Decagon lesson bites hard**: Decagon's cheaper hybrid at ₦550,000 failed *specifically because it was cheap and demanding* — learners "were reluctant to choose this more affordable option," preferring the expensive on-site course. In this market, low price plus high time demand reads as low value. **A hybrid programme must be positioned as the premium product, not the budget one. Discount by income tier and by volume, never by format.**

**Not higher than USD 500 for self-pay.** Above roughly USD 700 the programme converts from a self-pay product to an institutional-sale product in all three markets — a different business, with a six-to-twelve-month sales cycle and documented payment-lag risk in Nigeria (₦38 billion in health-worker allowance arrears is on the record). That is a legitimate business to be in, but it is not the one the January 2027 timetable supports.

### What this means for F024

The parameter currently declares a range of **USD 400–900** for the LMIC individual rung. The evidence supports **narrowing that to USD 450–600** and setting the working figure at **USD 500**.

The upper half of the declared range — USD 700–900 — is **above Pakistan's evidenced ceiling and at or above Nigeria's WACP-parity ceiling**. It should be removed from the range for the individual rung and retained only for the institutional rung, where the evidence supports it.

## 12. How the programme becomes self-sustaining

Price is not the lever. Four things are.

**Lever 1 — Pay faculty in CPD credit, not cash.** MDCN provider recognition costs roughly ₦300,000 (~USD 220) for the entire faculty body in year one and ~₦100,000 a year thereafter. Paying twelve faculty a modest honorarium for twelve sessions, plus coaches for sixty coaching calls at a forty-person cohort, would cost several thousand dollars a year at even the most conservative published rates. **The regulator has created a currency that costs the programme almost nothing to mint and that faculty are legally required to acquire.** This is the highest-leverage single move identified in the research, and it should be started immediately because provider recognition takes time.

**Lever 2 — Sell to institutions against the accreditation requirement, not to individuals against professional development.** ~~JCI QPS.01.00, Punjab Healthcare Commission Indicator 95, Sindh Healthcare Commission 4.5.16–4.5.18 and **SQHN's own HR.4.5** all require a *qualified* individual to guide quality.~~ **CORRECTED 3 August 2026 — see the correction in section 5.** JCI QPS.01.00, Punjab Healthcare Commission Indicator 95 and Sindh Healthcare Commission 4.5.16–4.5.18 do require a qualified individual. SQHN's HR.4.5 does not, and no Nigerian or Ghanaian regulator does. In the launch market this lever does not yet exist; it exists in Pakistan, which has been deferred to cohort 2. The lever must therefore be MADE — by SQHN amending QI 5.1 — before it can be sold against. The purchase is compliance, not discretion. Ghana's SafeCare evaluation shows what happens without that consequence: facilities resist the moment they are asked to pay. Add the ITF reimbursement route in Nigeria — subject to the primary verification flagged above — and the institutional sale becomes "recover money you have already paid to a levy you cannot avoid."

**Lever 3 — Build the credential-maintenance annuity from cohort 1.** At an ISQua-pattern USD 150 a year and an 84% steady-state renewal rate, this is the only revenue that compounds. It must be designed now — the obligation has to be in the cohort-1 promise, not retrofitted onto graduates who were told they were buying a one-off certificate.

**Lever 4 — Attack the in-person cost line, because it is 67–87% of implementation cost.** That means the observership geography parameter (F007) is not an administrative detail — it is the largest single determinant of whether the unit economics work. "Own city or own country" is materially cheaper than any centralised model, and the Host Site Standard is what makes it defensible.

**And one thing not to do.** Do not build the operating model on donor funding. Rwanda's Human Resources for Health programme lost PEPFAR — 60% of external funding — more than two years before its planned close, because it was reclassified as a "noncore" investment in a strategy change in Washington. In 2025 roughly 615 of 770 USAID global health projects closed. Grants may fund curriculum build, platform, accreditation and first-cohort scholarships. **No donor should fund a recurring operating cost, and no single funder should exceed about 30% of the cost base.** Write the cliff plan before the money lands.
